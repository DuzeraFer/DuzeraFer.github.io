Web game Node 24 · WebSocket Solo project

One film, five frames,
everyone in the room.

FrameDuels is a party game about guessing a film from a single still. One player picks the film and decides which frame the room gets to see; the other has five guesses, and each wrong one buys exactly one more frame. Phones are the controllers, a laptop or TV is the shared screen, and the whole thing runs on a Node server with two dependencies and no build step.

Play it

01The screen everyone watches

A browser on a TV or laptop opens /tv, types the four-letter room code, and becomes a spectator. It never knows more than the guesser does — the server builds a separate view per role, so the film stays secret even though the frame is on a screen both players face. Earlier frames line up underneath, and the scores update live.

02Phones do the playing

Joining is a QR code or four letters — no app, no account. The setter browses every usable still of their film and picks the one that gives the least away; a person reading a frame beats any classifier, so there is no blocklist. The guesser sees only what has been shown, with one new frame bought per wrong guess, and the points tile ticking down from five.

03The same game on a laptop

Every screen is the phone layout in a centred column, except the three where width actually helps — guessing, choosing a frame and picking a film go two-column at 900px and wider. Search is typed against TMDB and resolved to a film and a year, so "Aliens" and "Aliens vs Predator" never get confused, and a search that finds nothing costs no guess.

04Six modes, one rule engine

Adding modes meant generalising, not branching. 1 vs 1 became "two sides of one player", Team vs Team "two sides of up to six", and the scoring, timers and turn order never learned which one they were in. The server holds the rules; every client only draws a view it was handed.

Daily

The same five films for everyone, every day, with a score you can share.

Single player

Endless films on your own, against the bot that also covers timeouts.

1 vs 1

Two phones, three rounds each, roles swapping after every film.

Team vs Team

Up to six a side. A rotating captain plays; teammates search and send suggestions.

Free for all

Everyone guesses at once, and the room fills up as friends arrive.

Race

Health instead of points — wrong guesses cost, and the first side to run out loses.

The rules live on the server

A pure module holds the state, the legal actions and a per-role view. Nothing a client sends is trusted, which is also what makes a Unity port a client swap rather than a rewrite.

Rooms are memory, not a database

A room is an object that lives as long as the game. Sockets reconnect into their seat, so a phone that locks or drops Wi-Fi picks up where it left off.

Frames come from TMDB

Choosing an exact minute would need the films themselves, so frames are backdrops with no language tag and at least 1280px wide. Measured across tiers, that leaves roughly 24,000 films with ten or more usable stills.

The person is the filter

Some backdrops are promotional key art and give the film away instantly. Rather than detect them, the setter simply doesn't pick them — one decision that removed a whole subsystem.

Every screen is animated

Sheets, dialogs, chat, the dealt poster card, the flip and the reveal each have their own motion, written as one small shared module and paused when the tab is hidden.

Two languages, one room

English and Portuguese cover the interface and the film titles. A room has one language and any device in it can change it mid-game.

Grab a phone and a TV, and it just works.

This product uses the TMDB API but is not endorsed or certified by TMDB.
